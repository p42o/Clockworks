Shop scenario: a composite of situations common in Twin Cities shops. Not a real client. Names and numbers are illustrative, and your results will differ.

I'm Parker. I run MN Clockworks. Imagine an HVAC owner named Mike who wants a more manageable routine for weekly reports and customer email. I would start with the recurring writing tasks, rather than a broad technology overhaul.

## The starting point: Sunday nights at the kitchen table

In this hypothetical, Mike gathers job notes, revenue figures, crew issues, and plans for the coming week into a report for his business partner. He starts with a blank document each time.

He also rewrites follow-up emails and handles recurring questions from scratch. Seasonal maintenance reminders, after-hours service inquiries, and quote requests each need a response, but the structure often repeats.

I would separate the information that changes from the instructions that stay the same. The job details need Mike's judgment. The report format and email structure can live in reusable templates.

## The weekly report template

I would create a Claude Project called "Weekly Report" with standing instructions about the report's sections, its audience, and its format. A simple Google Doc could hold the input form: jobs completed, numbers, unresolved issues, and upcoming work.

Mike could fill in that form and ask Claude: "Turn these notes into this week's report. Use only the information provided. Flag missing details instead of filling them in."

I would start with the Claude free tier for this illustration.

Before sharing the draft, he would check the figures against his records, confirm job statuses, and remove anything that should not be shared. The template would organize his notes; he would remain responsible for the report.

## Reusable email prompts

I would draft prompts for quote follow-ups, new lead responses, post-job check-ins, after-hours calls, and seasonal maintenance outreach. Each could live in a shared Google Doc for reference.

For example: "Draft a follow-up about [estimate]. Use these details: [notes]. Ask whether the customer has questions. Do not invent availability, prices, or commitments."

Mike would replace the placeholders, check the customer details, and review the draft before sending it. A template should not turn an unanswered question into an invented answer.

## A rough-draft habit

For messages outside those templates, I would suggest writing a rough version first and asking Claude to clean it up while preserving the meaning. Mike could compare the draft with his notes and decide whether it still sounds like him.

This is a writing aid, not a reason to send messages without reading them. Requests involving an upset customer, an urgent repair, or a disputed bill would need particular attention.

## How I would evaluate it

I would compare the effort of preparing and reviewing drafts with the existing routine. I would also check whether the reports are accurate and whether the emails answer the customer's actual question.

This illustration does not establish time savings, leads, or revenue. If the templates require too much correction, I would revise them or leave that task manual.

The useful starting point is a repeatable input form, clear drafting instructions, and a human review step. Mike is fictional; those are concrete steps an owner can try on their own work.
